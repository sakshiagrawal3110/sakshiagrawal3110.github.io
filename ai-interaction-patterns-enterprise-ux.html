A Taxonomy for AI Interaction Patterns in Enterprise UX

Defining Scalable Interaction Models for Human-AI Collaboration

Role & scope

Role: Learner and co-member of the study
Timeline: 7 Months
Research Type: Systems Research + Framework Development
Domains: Enterprise SaaS, Analytics, Productivity, Operations
Outputs: Interaction Taxonomy, Pattern Library, Governance Model

Overview

As enterprise products rapidly integrated AI capabilities, interaction paradigms became fragmented and inconsistent across teams. Organizations lacked shared terminology, reusable AI patterns, governance structures, and interaction standards.

This project established a comprehensive taxonomy of AI interaction patterns designed to standardize human-AI collaboration across enterprise ecosystems.

Research objective

How should AI interaction patterns be systematically categorized to improve scalability, usability, trust, and governance in enterprise UX?

Product ecosystem audit

Analyzed 76 AI-enabled enterprise workflows, 24 SaaS platforms, 180+ interaction states, and 400+ AI touchpoints to identify recurring behavior and design needs.

Pattern mapping

Interaction patterns were clustered based on autonomy level, user agency, cognitive load, explainability requirements, temporal behavior, visibility, and control.

Stakeholder workshops

Facilitated alignment sessions with designers, AI engineers, PMs, compliance teams, and governance specialists to validate the taxonomy and adoption strategy.

Key discovery

Most enterprise AI experiences fell into recurring behavioral archetypes rather than unique product innovations. This revealed the opportunity to create scalable interaction primitives.

The taxonomy framework

The framework defined 12 AI interaction patterns grouped into four major categories, each with its own design principles and governance implications.

Category 1 — Ambient Intelligence

Low-friction passive assistance.

  • Anomaly detection
  • Predictive surfacing
  • Contextual reminders

Design principle: AI should remain peripheral until relevant.

Category 2 — Guided Decision Support

AI assists decision-making while preserving user agency.

  • Ranked recommendations
  • Scenario modeling
  • Prioritization engines

Design principle: Explain reasoning without overwhelming users.

Category 3 — Conversational Collaboration

Bidirectional interaction systems.

  • Copilots
  • Workflow assistants
  • Generative interfaces

Design principle: Maintain context continuity and controllability.

Category 4 — Autonomous Operations

AI performs actions proactively.

  • Workflow automation
  • Predictive execution
  • Background optimization

Design principle: Visibility and reversibility are critical.

Evaluation framework

Each interaction type was evaluated across trust requirements, cognitive load, operational risk, explainability depth, failure visibility, and intervention frequency.

Strategic contributions

  • Interaction governance models
  • Escalation heuristics
  • Transparency ladders
  • Autonomy thresholds
  • Trust calibration matrices

Organizational impact

The taxonomy was adopted by 12 product teams, 4 enterprise business units, and internal AI governance initiatives. It influenced AI design reviews, interaction standards, design system evolution, and enterprise AI compliance processes.

Strategic learnings

  • AI UX requires systems thinking: interactions cannot be designed as isolated screens.
  • Autonomy must be explicitly designed: users need clarity around control boundaries, accountability, and reversibility.
  • Enterprise AI requires governance layers: scalability depends on repeatable interaction standards.

Final reflection

This project shifted my perspective from designing individual AI features to designing interaction ecosystems. The taxonomy demonstrated that scalable AI UX requires behavioral frameworks, governance principles, trust-aware architecture, and systemic interaction modeling.
